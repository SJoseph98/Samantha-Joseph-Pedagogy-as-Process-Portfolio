Curriculum Integration and Collaboration

Collaborative Planning and Delivery of an Interdisciplinary Lesson on Dancehall

This report explains how I, a CSEC Music teacher, collaborated with a History/Social Studies colleague to plan and teach a 70-minute integrated lesson on Dancehall to three Form 4 students in a Roman Catholic school. It justifies the chosen integration model with academic literature and describes the evidence of our collaboration, which is included in this portfolio.

Selection and Justification of the Model

Drake and Burns (2004) describe three approaches to integrated curriculum. In multidisciplinary integration, subjects share a theme but keep separate content. In interdisciplinary integration, teachers organise learning around concepts and skills that overlap the standards of each subject. In transdisciplinary integration, real-world or student-generated questions drive learning and disciplinary boundaries recede. I first considered a transdisciplinary model but selected an interdisciplinary one for three reasons.

First, our learning targets were fixed by two syllabi: the CSEC Music – Music History objectives on stylistic elements and on historical, economic and socio-cultural contexts, and Theme 9 (Caribbean Society) in History/Social Studies. Interdisciplinary integration keeps each subject’s standards visible while identifying common ground (Drake & Burns, 2004; Jacobs, 1989). Transdisciplinary integration, by contrast, is typically built around learners’ own questions and concerns (Beane, 1997), which is difficult to achieve credibly in one 70-minute lesson addressing externally set objectives.

Second, Dancehall offers natural conceptual overlap. Its stylistic features, such as riddim, toasting and digital production, cannot be fully understood apart from Kingston’s sound system culture and the social and economic conditions of post-independence Jamaica (Hope, 2006; Stolzoff, 2000). Cooper (2004) shows that the “slackness” versus “culture” debate is also a debate about class, language and respectability, which is central to Theme 9. This overlap matches Fogarty’s (1991) shared model, in which two teachers plan around concepts and skills that emerge in both disciplines.

Third, the model supports coherent assessment. Because both subjects value justifying claims with evidence, one task could be judged against both subjects’ criteria. Our shared concept was that music reflects and shapes the society that produces it, and our essential question was, “How does the sound of Dancehall reflect the society that created it?”

Planning and Collaboration Process

We held three planning sessions between September 7 and 18, 2026, and kept a co-planning log. Across these sessions, we compared syllabus objectives side by side to locate overlap, agreed the shared concept, essential question and shared skills, and mapped each objective to a phase of the lesson so that neither subject was subordinated to the other. Together we also chose the songs and the specific versions to be used and discussed their lyrics in detail. Because we were teaching adolescents in a Roman Catholic school, suitability was a central criterion, and it shaped the discussion norms built into the lesson.

Roles reflected our expertise. I led the musical analysis and body-percussion activities, my colleague led the historical briefing and timeline, and we co-facilitated the integrated task, discussion and assessment. This follows the team-teaching approach described by Cook and Friend (1995), in which both teachers share responsibility for instruction rather than one observing. Resources were co-created: I prepared the Listening Guide, my colleague prepared the timeline and event cards, and we jointly wrote the Integrated Song Analysis Organizer, rubric and exit ticket.

Timetabling was a major challenge. Only one double period existed in which I taught the Form 4 class and my colleague was free to co-teach, so we had no alternative slot. On the first planned day, my colleague unexpectedly became ill and the lesson was postponed by a week. Because our plan, roles and resources were already documented and shared, we delivered the lesson as planned the following week.

Lesson Delivery and Alignment with the Model

The lesson sequence made integration visible. In Sections 1 and 2, each discipline had a distinct lens, preserving disciplinary integrity (Drake & Burns, 2004). Integration occurred through a collaborative cause-and-effect chart, where students physically linked musical features to historical causes with string and arrows, mirrored on a shared digital board. In Section 3, students completed the Integrated Song Analysis Organizer and wrote a claim requiring one piece of musical and one piece of historical evidence. The exit ticket and a dual-discipline rubric assessed the same integrated task, scored by both teachers.

Activities were multimodal, combining visual (wall chart, timeline), auditory (guided listening, discussion), kinaesthetic (body percussion, sequencing cards, a “take a stand” line) and digital elements. Because Pashler et al. (2008) found little evidence that matching instruction to fixed learning styles improves learning, I treated these as multiple routes to the same content for every student rather than as separate learner types.

Reflection and Limitations

The lesson was delivered successfully, and the students responded well to both the musical and the historical content. In my observation, they engaged with each teacher’s subject on its own terms and then drew on both to build their claims, which suggests that the two lenses complemented rather than competed with each other (Drake & Burns, 2004).

My colleague’s enthusiasm for the topic meant that the historical context ran longer than planned. Rather than shortening either subject, we adjusted the timing of subsequent sections together. This shared decision-making reflects the joint responsibility that Cook and Friend (1995) associate with team teaching, and it was possible because both of us understood the whole lesson rather than only our own phase. It also showed that interdisciplinary integration needs flexible time, since depth in one discipline can crowd out the other.

Limitations remain. Evidence of learning comes mainly from teacher observation of one lesson with three students, so it cannot be generalised. A single shared double period also left no opportunity to repeat or refine the lesson. A longer unit would allow more sustained collaboration and could invite students to pose their own questions, moving towards a transdisciplinary approach (Beane, 1997; Drake & Burns, 2004).

Agent of Change

Being an agent of change, for me, begins with a simple belief: music belongs to every student, not only to the ones who already play well. I teach at St. Joseph’s Convent, San Fernando, where I see daily how music shapes my students’ sense of identity, confidence and belonging. Many of them arrive in the pan room or at the keyboard convinced that they are “not musical.” What motivates me most is watching that belief change. When a student who hesitated to pick up the sticks finally plays her part with the section, I am reminded that my role is not to deliver a syllabus, but to open doors that students did not know were there.

My motivation also comes from the culture we live in. Steel pan, calypso, soca and parang are not just entertainment; they carry our history, struggles and creativity. I want my students to see Caribbean music as serious knowledge worthy of study, and I want my colleagues to see music as a subject that can strengthen learning across the curriculum.

The Postgraduate Diploma in Education programme has given me the tools to act on these motivations deliberately. Through task analysis, I have learnt to break complex musical skills into smaller, manageable steps, which has changed how I teach rhythm patterns and pan parts. Instead of expecting students to absorb a whole passage by ear, I now scaffold the learning: I model each section, check understanding and gradually release responsibility so that students gain independence. I have also become more intentional in lesson planning, aligning objectives, activities and assessment so that every part of a lesson has a clear purpose.

One of my most meaningful efforts has been co-teaching an interdisciplinary Dancehall lesson with Ms. Marcela Boatswain, our History and Social Studies teacher, for a Form 4 CSEC class. By connecting the musical features of Dancehall to its social and historical roots, students engaged more critically and saw how subjects speak to one another. This collaboration showed me that change does not have to happen alone. I hope to build on it by encouraging more cross-curricular partnerships and by sharing strategies such as scaffolding and task analysis with colleagues during department meetings.

I am committed to reflecting regularly on my practice, adapting to the realities of my teaching spaces and resources, and modelling a willingness to try new approaches. Being an agent of change means refusing to settle for “how it has always been done.” It means continually asking how I can make learning more relevant, inclusive and empowering, so that my students leave not only with musical skills, but with confidence in who they are.
