Section B - Unit and Lesson Planning (Alternative 1)

Caption

I chose calypso as the topic of this unit because it is part of my students' musical heritage, yet it is often treated as something to study or reproduce rather than create with. As a Trinidadian genre, calypso carries our history, social commentary and identity, and I wanted my students to engage with it as living music that belongs to them.

Integrating calypso into the CSEC arranging component lets students meet syllabus requirements through culturally meaningful music. Learning about calypso and performing existing songs has value, but it keeps students in the role of receivers. Arranging asks them to discover what makes calypso distinctive and use those elements to make their own musical decisions.

This matters especially in an all-girls school. Calypso has historically been dominated by men, yet women like Calypso Rose have claimed powerful voices within it. Through arranging, my students claim their own creative voice in the genre.

This shift from reproducing music to creating it is the heart of the unit. When students build an arrangement from their own understanding of a local genre, they develop confidence in their musical judgement and begin to see themselves as contributors to Caribbean music, not only its audience.

Task Analysis

Target skill: a Form Three class plays the given 8-bar piece together on the low C tenor pan, with correct notes, rhythm and dynamics at ♩ = 80.

1. Materials, Context and Prerequisites

Materials: low C tenor pans and sticks (one per student); the teacher’s pan for modelling; scores on stands; an enlarged score on the whiteboard; a metronome.

Context: a Form Three class of 18 students in the music room, in one 40-minute lesson. Students stand at their pans in a semicircle facing the teacher.

Prerequisites: students can already hold the sticks, hit and roll notes, and find note positions. They also need to read treble-clef notes in F major, know quavers, crotchets, minims and their rests, and understand f, p and decrescendo.

2. Component Skills

Any component skill not yet mastered is taught before the piece is attempted.

3. Teaching Sequence

4. Task Description

Students play an 8-bar melody in F major (4/4, ♩ = 80) on the low C tenor pan. It mixes single notes and double stops, with rolled minims, an off-beat entry in bar 2, a quaver passage that fades from f to p in bars 5–6, and a sudden f return after three beats’ rest in bar 7.

5. Steps

  1. The teacher plays the piece (M). Students name the notes, remembering B♭, then clap and count each bar from the board score (C, R).
  2. The teacher demonstrates each double stop. Students strike, then roll, each pair until both notes sound as one (M, R).
  3. Bars 5–6, the hardest section, come first at ♩ = 60. The teacher shows that bar 5 is one pattern repeated a step lower (M), counts in (P) and lowers a hand for the decrescendo (C).
  4. Bars 1–4: the teacher says “rest” on beat 1 of bar 2 and nods for the off-beat entry (C). Bars are repeated singly, then together (R).
  5. Bars 7–8: students count the three rests aloud, enter f on the teacher’s raised hand and stop on the cut-off (C, R).
  6. The sections are joined and played at ♩ = 70, then 80, while the teacher conducts and mouths note names (P, C, R).
  7. The teacher drops the note names, then the conducting, then gives only the count-in, until a student leads (F). Any group that struggles returns to the relevant step.

Instructional Objectives

Instructional Objectives: Lesson 4 of Unit Plan

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to:

  1. Aurally identify at least three different timbres in two calypso recordings.
  2. Describe two texture changes in a calypso excerpt using correct terminology.
  3. Compare two arrangements of the same calypso and explain how differences in instrumentation change the character of the music.
  4. Assign melody, harmony, bass and rhythm roles to group members, giving a reason for each choice based on range and playing ability.
  5. Create and perform a layered texture of at least three parts over eight bars, using steelpans, voice and/or body percussion.
  6. Adjust their own dynamic level while performing the layered texture so that the melody stays clearly audible.
  7. Respond respectfully to peers’ suggestions about layering and include at least one suggestion in the group’s final texture.
  8. Modify their own part to improve group balance.

Instructional Scaffolding

Definition

Instructional scaffolding is temporary, adjustable support that a teacher provides so learners can complete a task they cannot yet manage alone. The support is gradually withdrawn as competence grows, shifting responsibility for learning from teacher to student (Northern Illinois University [NIU], n.d.; Wood et al., 1976). It operates within Vygotsky's (1978) zone of proximal development: the gap between what a learner achieves independently and what they achieve with adult guidance or more capable peers.

Components of Contingent Scaffolding

Van de Pol et al. (2010) describe contingent scaffolding as three linked characteristics, all resting on ongoing diagnosis. Diagnosis: before and during the task, the teacher establishes what students can already do through questioning, observation or a short performance, locating each learner's zone of proximal development. Contingency: support is calibrated to that diagnosis. Wood et al. (1976) frame this as increasing help when a learner struggles and reducing it when they succeed. The four stages in the readings (the instructor does it, the class does it, the group does it, the individual does it) provide graded levels of support to move between (NIU, n.d.). Fading: support is withdrawn gradually as competence is shown, so students do not come to depend on it (NIU, n.d.). Transfer of responsibility: control of the task passes to the learner, who finishes working independently and can apply the skill in new contexts. Assessment between stages tells the teacher whether to advance or reinforce.

Flowchart

Concept (Lesson 2, Rhythmic Identity of Calypso): creating and performing a short calypso rhythmic ostinato.

Explanation of the Flowchart

The chart scaffolds a Lesson 2 outcome: students create and perform a short calypso rhythmic ostinato. It opens with diagnosis. A body-percussion warm-up shows whether students can hold a steady pulse, the prerequisite for feeling syncopation; if not, I reinforce the pulse first. In Stage 1, I model a calypso pattern on a sixteen-box rhythm grid (an advance organiser), counting aloud and thinking aloud about why accents fall off the beat. In Stage 2, the class echoes the pattern and completes the grid with me while I tap the pulse and point to boxes (verbal and visual prompts). Quick individual echoes check understanding. In Stage 3, small groups compose a two-bar ostinato on a partly completed grid, using rhythm cards and a cue card of terms. A confident student acts as section leader, and I offer hints only where a group stalls. Groups perform over a calypso recording and receive peer feedback. The decision diamond is the contingency point. Groups not yet secure loop back for more support, such as a fuller grid or a second model; secure groups move on with the grid removed. In Stage 4, each student creates and performs an ostinato from a blank grid, then explains in an exit ticket how syncopation shapes the calypso feel. Responsibility has now passed fully to the learner.
