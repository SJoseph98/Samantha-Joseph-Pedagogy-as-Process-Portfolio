Purpose Statement

This portfolio is a journal of my journey through the Postgraduate Diploma in Education programme. Through it, I hope to track my growth as a teacher and use it, when completed, to reflect on how my views and ideas at the start of this journey have changed at the end of the process. It represents not only a collection of academic tasks, but also a record of my evolving understanding of teaching, learning, and professional responsibility. By documenting my experiences and reflections over time, the portfolio allows me to examine how my perspectives develop as I engage with new knowledge, pedagogical theories, and practical teaching experiences.

This portfolio provides a structured space for reflection, self-assessment, and professional growth. Teaching is a dynamic and reflective profession that requires educators to continuously examine their beliefs, practices, and impact on students. This therefore functions as a tool for intentional reflection, encouraging me to critically consider my decisions, evaluate my progress, and identify areas for improvement. Through this process, I can recognize the ways in which my skills, confidence, and understanding of effective teaching have developed throughout the programme.

Additionally, my portfolio represents my commitment to becoming a thoughtful and reflective educator. It highlights my willingness to learn, adapt, and grow in response to new experiences and challenges. Ultimately, it serves as both a record of my development during the programme and a foundation for my continued growth as an educator dedicated to meaningful and effective teaching practice.

Letter to Self

Dear Samantha,

You decided to pursue the Postgraduate Diploma in Education programme last year and I know that as the process goes on, you may feel overwhelmed and want to quit. When that happens, remember why you chose teaching. You never wanted to teach, or at least that’s what you told yourself. Remember, you’ve been finding opportunities to help people learn your entire life. From those early primary school days teaching your teddy bears and the crash course sessions in the corridors in secondary school to the informal, but still very structured, “tutorials” in university for your peers, you have been drawn to teaching. The mentors and friends in your life have always seen the teacher in you but it took you a while to see that teaching was more than a career choice, it was, and is, a calling. Never forget that and never give up on your students. They need you more than you realise.

As for the DipEd programme, you need to continue to grow as a teacher. The journey is never complete. There is always more for you to know and to learn. You did not choose this programme because it is necessary for upward movement in the teaching service. You have always wanted to be “just” a teacher. One day, you may have a part to play in middle management, but today, your goal is to become the best teacher you can be. You want to be the teacher that reaches every student in some way, that makes school fun, and that helps students to become the best version of themselves. This programme is a tool to make that step forward.

The DipEd programme is not everything you need to know. It is important to remember that there is still more to learn after this is finished. But I hope that it will help you to become more competent and confident in teaching those areas of your curriculum that you feel apprehension towards. I also hope that it will give you greater insights into the whys of teaching and learning, not just the hows. Finally, I hope that at the end of this programme you come out a more mature and better equipped teacher, albeit a little tired.

You will want to quit. You feel it already as you’re writing this letter, but you have to remember that it will all be worth it in the end.

17 Hardial Avenue,
St. Augustine South.

8th March 2026

Your past self,
Samantha Joseph

Statement of Initial Philosophy

The music classroom is a recording studio and I, the teacher, am the sound engineer. A sound engineer does not create music independently of the musicians or expect every instrument to sound alike. Instead, the engineer listens carefully, adjusts the levels, creates balance, and continually responds to what is happening so that every instrument can contribute to the final cohesive recording. My role in the classroom is not to produce identical learners, but to create the conditions in which each student can develop and make a meaningful contribution.

Every student enters my classroom as a different “track” with unique experiences, abilities, interests, and ways of learning. Some are confident performers while others communicate more readily through movement, listening, composing, or discussion. Howard Gardner’s Theory of Multiple Intelligences supports my belief that students have different modalities through which they can access and demonstrate learning. Like a sound engineer adjusting the levels of individual tracks, I must therefore listen to my students and adapt my instructional approaches to respond to their varying needs. This does not mean lowering expectations but rather, providing appropriate pathways for each student to meaningfully engage with learning. Equity is not giving every student the same “volume.” Equity is giving each student the support necessary to be heard.

I believe that effective music education must produce a complete musical picture. In music education, a major goal is learning to play an instrument and to “create” music. However, it must extend beyond performance, exposing the child to playing, creating, improvising, theory, literacy, history, aural skills, form, shape, etc. Consequently, I prioritize active learning approaches in which students listen, perform, move, create, question, and discover. I incorporate visual, auditory, and kinaesthetic experiences because musical understanding can be developed through multiple pathways. Discovery learning is also important to me because students should sometimes be given the opportunity to find musical possibilities rather than simply being told what to think or do.

A recording is never created by one track alone. I see my classroom as an ensemble and a community in which students listen to one another, support one another, take risks, and understand that their individual contributions affect the whole. I want students to feel safe enough to experiment and express themselves freely.

Assessment, too, is a form of listening. Just as an engineer listens back to a recording to identify what needs adjustment, I use assessment to understand student progress and evaluate the effectiveness of my teaching. I value varied assessments, particularly creative and sustained projects that allow students to demonstrate both knowledge and individuality.

Ultimately, I do not want my students simply to leave with a completed “recording” of musical knowledge. I want the studio to become a space where they discover their voices, learn to hear others, and experience music as a means of expression, connection, and personal growth. My greatest responsibility is to create the conditions in which every student can be heard—and where music can help shape their lives.
