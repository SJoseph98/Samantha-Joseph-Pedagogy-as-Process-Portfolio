This section contains my explanation of two Classroom Assessment Standards, my critique of a Form 4 examination I set, a comparison of performance and traditional assessment, and a calypso performance task with its rubric, rationale, and discussion of benefits and challenges. Standards of Classroom Assessment

Assessment Design and Reliability and Validity: Two Classroom Assessment Standards

The Classroom Assessment Standards for PreK-12 Teachers (Klinger et al., 2015), published by the Joint Committee on Standards for Educational Evaluation, describe sound assessment practice in three domains: foundations, use, and quality. This essay explains two of these standards: Assessment Design, from the foundations domain, and Reliability and Validity, from the quality domain. Both concern whether an assessment gives teachers trustworthy evidence of what students have learned.

The Assessment Design standard holds that the types and methods of assessment a teacher chooses should allow students to clearly demonstrate the learning being targeted (Klinger et al., 2015). Good design therefore begins with alignment. Biggs (1996) described constructive alignment as matching teaching activities and assessment tasks to intended learning outcomes, so that students must perform the very skills they were meant to learn. A table of specifications supports this by mapping each item to an outcome and a cognitive level. The revised taxonomy of Anderson and Krathwohl (2001), which ranges from remembering to creating, helps teachers check whether tasks demand more than recall when outcomes call for application or analysis.

Design also covers how items are written and presented. Clear directions, unambiguous wording, accurate labelling, and adequate answer space let students focus on the content rather than on decoding the task. Haladyna et al. (2002) reviewed item-writing guidance and found strong agreement that ambiguous or unclear wording should be avoided; although their review focused on multiple-choice items, this principle applies to any format. Popham (2017) similarly warns that poorly constructed items can prevent students from showing what they actually know.

The Reliability and Validity standard requires classroom assessment practices to produce consistent, dependable information that supports appropriate interpretations and decisions about students (Klinger et al., 2015). Validity is the degree to which evidence supports the interpretations and uses of results (Messick, 1989). Messick identified two main threats: construct underrepresentation, where an assessment omits important parts of what it intends to measure, and construct-irrelevant variance, where scores are affected by unrelated factors such as confusing instructions. Kane (2013) adds that the intended interpretation should be stated explicitly and the evidence for each part of it checked.

Reliability concerns the consistency of results. Brookhart (2003) argued that measurement theory built for large-scale testing fits classrooms poorly, and Smith (2003) proposed that classroom reliability be judged by whether a teacher has sufficient information to make a sound decision about each student. Scoring is a major source of inconsistency in open-ended tasks. In their review of rubric research, Jonsson and Svingby (2007) found that analytic rubrics, particularly when combined with exemplars and rater training, can improve scoring consistency.

The two standards are closely linked. Careful design aligns tasks with learning outcomes and removes irrelevant sources of difficulty, which makes valid and reliable interpretations possible. Together, they help teachers make fair, defensible decisions about student learning.

Critique of an Assessment and Suggestions for Improvement

Link to Critiqued Assessment

  • Critiqued Assessment: Term 1 Form 4 End of Term Music Examination (PDF)

I chose to critique a Term 1 Form 4 End of Term Music Examination that I created in 2024, against two Classroom Assessment Standards (Klinger et al., 2015): Assessment Design and Reliability and Validity. This exam was used as a summative assessment to evaluate students’ competence in the subject after their first term in form 4. The paper is a 90-minute written exam with two sections: History (28 marks) and Theory (60 marks). The students were expected to answer all questions.

On reflection, my examination partly conforms to the assessment design standard. Its structure is clear, with labelled sections and marks shown for every item. In the Theory section, I used notation tasks suited to the skills tested, from rewriting melodies in a new metre to harmonic analysis. These tasks require my students to apply and analyse, not merely remember (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001).

However, several design flaws may have limited my students' ability to show their learning. My general instructions refer to "filling in your new choice," wording suited to multiple-choice tests, although the paper has no such items, and they urge students to work "as quickly" as they can, which can stress speed over accuracy. In Section I, Question 3, I labelled the four composers a, b, c, and b. Some of my prompts are vague: "explain the use of" four characteristics and "key features of their careers" leave the expected answer open. In Theory Question 2, I did not state the range or direction of the scales, and in Question 3b, I did not specify the note around which the melody should be inverted. Haladyna et al. (2002) caution that such ambiguity introduces avoidable difficulty.

Validity is mixed. The Theory items closely match the skills they name, so I can interpret those scores with some confidence. Section I, however, relies entirely on written recall. My students never heard or saw any music, so the section reflects what they memorised rather than their ability to recognise stylistic features. If my intended outcome is stylistic understanding, this is construct underrepresentation (Messick, 1989). My labelling errors and vague wording also add construct-irrelevant variance, as students may have lost marks through confusion.

However, reliability is the weaker area. I did not create a marking scheme because I was the only teacher marking the papers, yet 48 of the 88 marks come from open-ended items requiring judgement: the three History questions and the 20-mark harmonic analysis. Being the sole marker removes disagreement between markers, but it does not guarantee that I applied the same criteria to the first script and the last. Without written mark allocations, I had no fixed reference to keep my judgements consistent. The Beethoven item illustrates the risk: he is widely regarded as a transitional figure between the Classical and Romantic eras, and I had not decided in advance which answers to accept.

In order to improve this assessment, I can use a table of specifications and a colleague review.

In reworking this paper, I will map each item to a stated learning outcome and cognitive level in a table of specifications. This will show me that Section I tests only recall (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001; Biggs, 1996). I would replace one History item with a short score extract or recorded excerpt that asks students to identify and justify Baroque or Classical features. I will also ask a colleague to review my draft so that errors like those above are corrected before students sit the paper.

Another avenue for improvement is developing an analytic marking scheme and moderating marking.

Even as the only marker, writing an analytic marking scheme for each open-ended item, stating how marks are divided and which answers are acceptable, ensures that all scripts are marked consistently. For example, History Question 1 could award one mark per characteristic and one per explanation, and the analysis could mark the key, each Roman numeral, and each chord symbol separately. I would also list acceptable alternatives, such as either era for Beethoven. I could also re-mark a sample of scripts to check my own consistency. Jonsson and Svingby (2007) found that analytic rubrics, especially with exemplars and rater training, support more consistent scoring, which would give me sufficient information for sound decisions about each student (Smith, 2003).

Overall, my examination is well structured, and its Theory section largely conforms to both standards. Its main weaknesses are ambiguous wording, recall-only History items, and the absence of a marking scheme. These two changes would bring my future assessments closer to both standards.

Criteria Distinguishing Performance From Traditional Assessment

Performance assessment requires students to demonstrate their learning by carrying out a task or creating a product, which the teacher observes and judges against set criteria (Stiggins, 1987). Traditional assessment usually refers to paper-and-pencil tests of selected-response or short-answer items scored against an answer key (Wiggins, 1990). Three criteria distinguish the two.

The first is the type of response students give. Traditional items ask students to choose or recall an answer, which shows whether they recognise correct information but not whether they can use it. Performance assessment requires students to construct their own response, which shows how they apply knowledge and skill together. In my unit, the Lesson 2 written identification task shows whether students can name syncopation, while creating and performing a rhythmic ostinato shows whether they can produce it.

The second is authenticity. Traditional tests present decontextualised items with a single correct answer, measuring knowledge apart from how it is used. Performance assessment instead uses tasks that replicate the challenges practitioners face, requiring judgement in context and allowing several valid solutions (Wiggins, 1990). In my unit, the Lesson 1 listening worksheet asks students to identify calypso features, while the arrangement task requires them to work as arrangers, adapting a melody for available steelpans and voices.

The third is how responses are scored. Traditional items are marked right or wrong against a key. Because performance tasks allow many acceptable responses, they must be judged against explicit criteria, usually set out in a rubric that describes levels of quality (Brookhart, 2013; Linn et al., 1991). When shared in advance, these criteria also guide students' work over time rather than only grading a single sitting. My unit uses rehearsal checklists, peer feedback forms, and a summative performance rubric to judge both the process and the final arrangement.

Performance Assessment Task and Rubric

Performance Assessment Task

For the Calypso Unit presented in Section B, a performance assessment can be created that involves students using the skills and information they have learnt to create a calypso arrangement of a non-calypso song.

Concept assessed: An arrangement is created by making intentional decisions about how existing musical material is transformed, and calypso style comes from how those elements interact (Lessons 5–8).

Learning outcomes assessed (from the unit plan): Students will be able to:

  • incorporate at least two manipulated musical elements into an arrangement;
  • demonstrate appropriate calypso stylistic characteristics;
  • maintain an independent part, respond to cues, and keep ensemble balance;
  • explain significant creative decisions and identify areas for further development.

CSEC Music profiles addressed: Composing and Arranging, Performing, and Listening and Appraising.

Students will be given a task sheet that presents the assignment and the rubric for assessment in Lesson 6. The task sheet is shown below:

Calypso Assignment

Scenario: The Music Department is presenting a Caribbean Music Showcase at the end of term. Your group has been invited to take a familiar melody and turn it into a short calypso arrangement for the audience.

What you will do:

  1. In groups of four or five, choose a short melody (about 16–32 bars) from the approved list of folk songs.
  2. Arrange it as a calypso lasting 1½ to 3 minutes, with an introduction, at least two contrasting sections, and an ending.
  3. Manipulate at least two musical elements, for example, syncopated rhythm, bass line and chord accompaniment, call and response, texture, or dynamics.
  4. Make sure every group member performs an independent part on steelpan, voice, keyboard, guitar, or percussion (including body percussion).
  5. Submit your arrangement blueprint: a structure map, chord chart, and list of part assignments. You may use staff notation, graphic notation, or MuseScore.
  6. Perform your arrangement live for the class. The performance will be recorded.
  7. Take part in a short viva voce after your performance. Each member will answer at least one question, such as "Which element did you change, and how does that change make the piece sound like calypso?"

Learning Goals and Assessment Alignment

I want my students to understand three things. First, calypso's identity comes from the interaction of its musical elements, such as syncopated rhythm, bass and accompaniment patterns, call and response, and steelpan timbre, with its Caribbean cultural context. Second, arranging is purposeful transformation: every change to rhythm, harmony, texture, or structure should have a musical reason. Third, strong performances develop through rehearsal, feedback, and revision.

The performance assessment supports these goals because students cannot complete it by recalling facts. To make a familiar melody sound like calypso, they must apply what they know about its features, which shows me whether they understand how the style works rather than only what it is called. Requiring at least two manipulated elements and a clear structure makes purposeful transformation concrete, and the viva voce asks each student to explain why the group made its choices, revealing reasoning that a performance alone might hide.

The rubric helps in two ways. Its criteria mirror these understandings, so sharing it in Lesson 6 shows students what quality looks like before they begin. In Lesson 7, groups use it to give peer feedback on recorded drafts and revise before the final performance, which builds the third understanding directly. Separating group and individual criteria also lets me see whether each student, not only the group, has reached the intended understanding.

Benefits and Challenges of Administering the Rubric and Performance Assessment

Administering the Calypso Remix performance assessment and its rubric offers clear benefits for my students' learning, but it also presents practical and technical challenges that I must plan for.

One benefit is that the rubric makes the expectations of a complex task visible before students begin. Andrade (2000) argues that rubrics used as teaching tools help students think about quality and improve their own work, and Panadero and Jonsson (2013) found that rubrics used formatively can support self-assessment and self-regulation, although the effect depends on how they are introduced. Because my students use the rubric to give peer feedback on recorded drafts in Lesson 7, it functions as a learning tool and not only as a grading tool.

A second benefit is that the task produces more valid evidence of musical learning than a written test. It captures skills that cannot be shown on paper, such as maintaining an independent part, responding to cues, and making stylistic decisions. Darling-Hammond and Adamson (2010) argue that performance assessments measure higher-order skills such as application, problem-solving, and communication more directly than selected-response tests. Since my unit's goals centre on arranging and performing, this evidence matches what I intend students to learn.

These benefits come with challenges, the first of which is consistency of scoring. Music performance is fleeting, and scoring it involves judgement. Wesolowski (2012) notes that music performance assessment is prone to subjectivity and that rubrics with clearly defined criteria are needed to make judgements more consistent, yet Jonsson and Svingby (2007) found that rubrics alone do not guarantee reliable scoring; consistency improves when they are combined with exemplars and rater training. As the only marker, I could also let a group's overall impression influence individual scores. To reduce this, I will record every performance, mark from the recordings, use anchor examples for each level, and re-mark a sample to check my consistency.

A second challenge involves time, logistics, and generalisability. Performance assessments take far more time to administer and score than written tests (Linn et al., 1991). Rehearsals, live performances, and individual viva questions will fill most of Lessons 7 and 8, and access to steelpans and practice space may be uneven across groups. Shavelson et al. (1993) also found that students' scores vary considerably from one performance task to another, so a single performance offers limited evidence, and a strong group may mask a weaker individual. I will therefore schedule instrument time in advance, keep the viva brief but individual, and combine the final score with evidence gathered earlier in the unit.

Overall, the benefits outweigh the challenges, provided I plan carefully for consistent scoring and efficient use of class time.
